🛡️ Mandatory protocol: from nominal optimum to a manufacturable, robust design

Decision rule: a stack that is optically excellent at nominal values but impossible to deposit, too thin, too complex, or too sensitive is rejected. Every industrial conclusion must separate nominal performance, manufacturability, and statistical yield.

  1. Specify before optimizing: window, incidence, polarization, quantity, and acceptance threshold.
  2. Audit the nominal stack: number of layers and materials, $d_{min}$, $d_{max}$, total thickness, and deposition order.
  3. Declare the process model: $\Delta d$, minimum thickness, and the layers actually perturbed.
  4. Declare the acceptance tolerances: without them the yield is undefined — it is the fraction of draws that stay inside the specification.
  5. Make the study reproducible: record the random seed; compare two designs using the same seed.
  1. Size the sample: $N=100$ for exploration, $N=500$ for comparison, and $N=1000$ for educational qualification.
  2. Report robust statistics: yield, 95% Wilson CI, mean, sample standard deviation, and P05/P50/P95.
  3. Use the adverse percentile: P95 for a cost to be minimized; P05 for a performance to be maximized.
  4. Decide: qualified, conditionally qualified, or not qualified, with margins and assumptions.

Warning: even 1000 successes out of 1000 do not prove a true 100% yield. The displayed confidence interval captures sampling uncertainty; it does not cover index uncertainty, thermal drift, roughness, or unmodelled defects.

Classic trap: the displayed P05/P50/P95 percentiles cover only the manufacturable realizations. They are therefore conditional. The yield, by contrast, is reported over the total number of runs. Never compare the two without saying so.

Theoretical Background: Interference & Transfer Matrix Method

💡 Physical Principle: Coatings rely on multiple beam interference. Optical path differences govern reflection and transmission. The core metric is the Optical Thickness ($n \times d$).

For each layer $i$ with thickness $d_i$ and complex index $N_i = n_i - ik_i$, the optical phase shift is:

$$\phi_i = \frac{2\pi d_i}{\lambda} \sqrt{N_i^2 - N_0^2 \sin^2\theta_0}$$

The characteristic transfer matrix of layer $i$ is:

$$M_i = \begin{pmatrix} \cos \phi_i & \frac{i}{\eta_i} \sin \phi_i \\ i\eta_i \sin \phi_i & \cos \phi_i \end{pmatrix}$$
⚡ Polarization States (Incidence $\theta > 0$):
  • S-Polarization (TE): $\vec{E} \perp$ plane of incidence $\implies \eta_i = \gamma_i = \sqrt{N_i^2 - N_0^2\sin^2\theta_0}$.
  • P-Polarization (TM): $\vec{E} \parallel$ plane of incidence $\implies \eta_i = N_i^2 / \gamma_i$.
  • Unpolarized light: $R_{avg} = \frac{R_s + R_p}{2}$ and $T_{avg} = \frac{T_s + T_p}{2}$.
📐 Ordering convention — applies throughout this lab:

Layers are listed in deposition order, substrate → superstrate. Layer 1 touches the substrate; the last one touches the air. Light therefore encounters them in reverse order, and the total matrix is $M = M_m \cdots M_2 M_1$.

III. Single-layer antireflection coating ⏱ 60 min indicative

Direct entry — prerequisites: single-interface reflection, optical phase $2\pi nd/\lambda$ and elementary complex matrix calculation. Complete at least Q5 in Exercise II if Fresnel coefficients are not yet secure.
💡 AR principle: quarter-wave condition $n \cdot d = \lambda_0 / 4$ with $n_{layer} = \sqrt{n_{substrate} \cdot n_{inc}}$.
Q10★★☆

Manually add the theoretical material AR (n=1.225) with $d = 112.3\,\text{nm}$. Find the spectral window where $R < 0.5\%$. Prove that $112.3\,\text{nm}$ is indeed the quarter-wave thickness at $550\,\text{nm}$ for this index.

🧠 Deliberate trap, to be exposed: the supplied index is exactly $1.22474 = \sqrt{1.50}$. But real BK7 has $n=1.5185$ at 550 nm, so the ideal index would be $\sqrt{1.5185}$. Compute that ideal index, the corresponding quarter-wave thickness, and then the residual $R$ actually obtained at 550 nm with the supplied material. Is it rigorously zero? Conclude: under what condition may one write "perfect antireflection"?
Q11★★☆

Does substrate dispersion actually matter? First record $n_{BK7}$ at 400, 550 and 750 nm: the variation exceeds 1%. One therefore expects an effect.

  • Carefully note the position of the $R$ minimum and the bounds of the $R<0.5\%$ range with the BK7 substrate.
  • Replace the substrate with Custom index at a constant $n = 1.5185$, and repeat both measurements.
🧠 The difference is below one nanometre — hence negligible. This is not an error: it is the result. Explain it. Hint: the position of the minimum is set by the phase condition $n_c d = \lambda/4$, which involves only the layer — non-dispersive here. Substrate dispersion acts solely on the amplitude condition $n_c=\sqrt{n_s}$, hence on the depth of the minimum, not on its wavelength.

Conclude by naming, within this lab, two situations where dispersion does become visible: you have met one already in Exercise I, and you will meet the other in Exercise VI.

Q12★★★

Demystifying the Black Box in Excel: Compute reflection $R$ of this layer at $550\,\text{nm}$ in Excel.

  • Compute the optical phase $\phi_1$.
  • Build matrix $M_1$ using =COMPLEX(real, imag) and =IMPRODUCT(z1, z2).
  • Compute the equivalent admittance $Y=\frac{m_{10}+\eta_s m_{11}}{m_{00}+\eta_s m_{01}}$ then $R=\left|\frac{\eta_0-Y}{\eta_0+Y}\right|^2$.
  • Check that you recover the simulator value.
🔍 Self-check: your $R$ must match the simulator to better than 0.001 point. If you get a negative $R$ or one above 1, the error is in the squared modulus. If the gap is of order one percent, check the sign of $i$ in the off-diagonal terms of $M_1$.

Q13★☆☆
Add two layers ($ZnS$ then $YF_3$). Manually adjust thicknesses $d_{ZnS}$ and $d_{YF3}$ ($< 150\,\text{nm}$) to minimize $R$ at $550\,\text{nm}$. Q14★★☆

2D Mapping in Excel: Explore the parameter landscape topology. At $\lambda = 550\,\text{nm}$:

  • Record $R$ on a grid: $d_{ZnS}$ from 60 to 140 nm and $d_{YF3}$ from 80 to 160 nm, in 20 nm steps — 25 readings. These bounds are not arbitrary: they span roughly one oscillation period of $R$. A coarser step would under-sample the landscape and the valley would vanish.
  • Enter the table into Excel and create a 3D Surface or Contour Plot.
  • Locate the coordinates of the minimum valley.
  • Is your Q13 minimum inside that valley? Is there more than one valley?
Q15★★★

First robust qualification — but over which window?

🧠 Specification trap, to be dealt with first. In 🎓 Student mode, exercise IV, the Mean R merit is offered by default over $400-750\,\text{nm}$. But you have just designed an antireflection coating at 550 nm. Read the merit value over that wide window: it is catastrophic. Is your design bad, or is your specification off-topic? Justify, then correct the window.
  • Set the AR window to 520–580 nm and the threshold to 0.8% (fields below the merit selector). Check that the nominal design now meets the criterion, and with what margin.
  • Qualify: $d_{min}=1$ nm, $N=500$. Compare $\Delta d = 3$ and $5\,\text{nm}$; the seed being fixed, both runs use the same realizations.
  • Report yield, the 95% Wilson CI and P95 of mean reflectance in both cases. Do the two confidence intervals overlap? Can you claim $\pm5$ is significantly worse, or is it sampling noise?
  • Look at P95 at $\pm5\,\text{nm}$: it exceeds the threshold while the mean respects it. What does that gap mean concretely for a production batch?
  • Isolate the critical layer — by hand, not with Monte-Carlo. The draw always perturbs both layers: locking applies to BFGS only, never to manufacturing. So work by sensitivity instead: from the nominal design, add $+3\,\text{nm}$ to ZnS alone and record the merit; return to nominal, add $+3\,\text{nm}$ to YF₃ alone and record. Which one degrades it more? Relate your answer to the index and the optical thickness $n\cdot d$ of each layer.
🔍 Self-check: with the right window, the yield at $\Delta d=5$ nm must be lower than or equal to the one at $\Delta d=3$ nm — that ordering is the only guarantee. If you get 0% everywhere, the merit window is still 400–750 nm. If you get 100% in both cases, your nominal margin is too comfortable for the test to discriminate: tighten the threshold (try 0.3% instead of 0.8%) and rerun both. A protocol that separates nothing is not a protocol.

A standard deviation without an acceptance criterion is not a robustness conclusion.

V. Multilayer synthesis & the Needle method ⏱ 90 min indicative

Direct entry — prerequisites: be able to define a merit function, distinguish global exploration from local refinement, and build a simple AR coating. Exercises III and IV are recommended; Q14 may be skipped if local minima are already understood.
Q16★★☆

BFGS local-convergence study. In 🎓 Student mode, exercise V, activate the Mean R merit over 400–750 nm with a 0.5% threshold. Goal: a broadband antireflection coating on BK7 using ZnS/YF₃.

🧠 Start with the instructive mistake. First record the merit of bare glass (no layer): that is your baseline. Then build the most natural stack — quarter-wave layers H L, then H L H L. The merit is two to three times WORSE than bare glass. This is not a handling error. Explain it: from an interference standpoint, what does a quarter-wave high-index layer on glass actually do? Must an antireflection coating be a quarter-wave stack?
  • Restart from thin, asymmetric layers (a few tens of nanometres), not quarter-waves. The optimizer is a local descent: it reaches the minimum of the valley you drop it into, and looks for no other.
  • Run it from at least three different initial stacks and record, each time, the initial merit, the final merit and the layer count after cleanup. Since the algorithm is deterministic, the same start returns exactly the same result: every bit of spread you observe comes from the starting point, not from the algorithm.
  • How many layers are needed? Find the best result you obtain with 2, then 3, then 4 layers. For each case, decide whether the 0.5% threshold is crossed and quantify the margin.
  • A starting point that stays stuck far above the others tells you nothing about the optimizer — there is only one, and it is deterministic. It tells you about that starting point. Verify this by rerunning from a different initial stack.
The exact merit grid is displayed below the selector. Copy it into your report: without it, your value is not reproducible by a third party.
Q17★★★

Guided Synthesis via the Needle Method:

  • Start with a single layer: YF3 with physical thickness $150\,\text{nm}$ on BK7 glass.
  • Click 📍 Analyze Needle. Read the diagnostic carefully: what material and depth $z$ does the perturbation analysis recommend?
  • The diagnostic numerically plots $\pi(z)=[J_0-J_{\delta}(z)]/\delta$ every $5\,\text{nm}$ using a virtual needle $\delta=0.1\,\text{nm}$. Locate its positive maximum and the position $z$ from the substrate, then insert the recommended needle.
  • Click Insert needle: the software creates the virtual $0.1\,\text{nm}$ layer and then automatically runs BFGS. Compare the merit immediately before insertion and after this refinement.
  • Critical analysis: explain the value of the two-step workflow (nucleation diagnostic $\to$ local refinement).
🧠 Three limitations to identify yourself (they are not displayed): (a) the scan is discrete, every 5 nm — what if the optimum lay 2.5 nm from a tested point? (b) $\pi(z)$ is never evaluated at the interfaces, although that is often where the true optimum sits: why this choice, and what is missed? (c) the derivative is approximated by finite differences with a finite $\delta$. Quantify at least one of these three limitations with a numerical test.

VI. Bragg mirrors: BK7 / (HL)^n H / Air ⏱ 55 min indicative

Direct entry — prerequisites: QWOT, constructive interference and spectrum reading. Exercise III is sufficient; Exercises IV and V are not required.
💡 Bragg mirror principle: constructive interference from alternating quarter-wave layers ($n \cdot d = \lambda_0 / 4$).
Q18★★☆

Enter formula (HL)^3 H at $\lambda_0 = 550\,\text{nm}$. Measure peak reflectance $R_{max}$ and record both edges of the stopband — the wavelength range over which the mirror reflects strongly, also called the photonic band gap — using $R \ge 90\%$ as the convention. Note the width you obtain.

🧠 Do not rush to the formula. Compare your measured width with the prediction $\Delta\lambda = \frac{4\lambda_0}{\pi}\arcsin\frac{n_H-n_L}{n_H+n_L}$ computed in Q4: the gap is large, of order one third. This is not a measurement error. Look at $R_{max}$: by how much does it exceed your 90% threshold? What happens to the measured width if $R_{max}$ barely clears the threshold? Formulate a hypothesis — you will test it in Q19.
🔑 Result to keep: the stopband edges govern the whole of Exercise IX — a measurement band placed outside this interval does not measure the filter, it measures the mirror's leakage. You will refine them in Q19 on a thicker mirror.
Q19★★☆

Compare (HL)^3 H (7 layers), (HL)^4 H (9), (HL)^5 H (11) and (HL)^6 H (13) using Save curve overlays. For each, record $R_{max}$ and the width at $R \ge 90\%$. Build the table of the four pairs.

🧠 The central result of this exercise. Both quantities saturate, but not in the same way. Now confront your four widths with the Q4 prediction: it describes none of your four mirrors exactly, yet it describes very well the one they converge towards. So what does that formula physically represent — the width of a real mirror, or a limit? Is your Q18 hypothesis confirmed?

Conclude: from how many periods onwards can the formula be used as an acceptable approximation? That is the value you will use in Q23 to place your measurement bands.

Q20★★☆

The outermost layers: a setting, not an improvement. On the 13-layer mirror, widen the display to $360-800\,\text{nm}$ so you can see the ripples on both sides of the stopband.

  • Reference: record three numbers — $R_{max}$ inside the band, the highest sidelobe on the blue side ($360-450\,\text{nm}$) and the highest sidelobe on the red side ($660-800\,\text{nm}$).
  • Set layers 1 and 13 to $0.5\,\text{QWOT}$ and repeat the three measurements.
  • Now set them to $1.3\,\text{QWOT}$ and repeat again.
🧠 What you will discover, and it is not what one expects. The ripples are not suppressed: they are transferred from one side of the stopband to the other. Thinning the outermost layers cleans one side and degrades the other; thickening them does the opposite. Explain this in terms of admittance matching — the admittance $\eta$ from the theory reminders: what does the wave see on arriving at the first interface, depending on the thickness of the outermost layer?

Finally look at $R_{max}$: does it change much? Conclude on the nature of this setting — is it a performance gain, or a trade-off the designer chooses according to which side of the spectrum must stay clean? Give an example of a component where this choice is forced.

🔍 Self-check: if both sidelobes move in the same direction, you have measured only one side of the band, or your display range is too narrow.

VII. Interference bandpass filter (Fabry–Pérot) ⏱ 75 min indicative

🎯 Deposited at Fresnel
Direct entry — prerequisites: Bragg mirror, resonant cavity and manual spectral-width measurement. Complete Exercise VI or review the Bragg reminder first; optimizer knowledge is not required.
💡 Fabry–Pérot cavity: two Bragg mirrors separated by a central half-wave spacer ($2H$ or $2L$). The more reflective the mirrors, the higher the finesse and the narrower the peak.
Q21★★☆

At $\lambda_0=550\,\text{nm}$, enter (HL)^2 2H (LH)^2, then (HL)^3 2H (LH)^3, then (HL)^4 2H (LH)^4. Measure the full width at half maximum of each.

Then test the cavity order: replace the 2H spacer with 4H (order $q=2$) then 6H ($q=3$). Notation: the prefix is the QWOT multiple, so 4H = 4 quarter-waves = 2 half-waves.

Establish the relationship between the number of mirror periods $n$, the cavity order $q$, and the resolving power $\lambda/\Delta\lambda$. Which of the two parameters acts most strongly?

Q22★★☆

Fresnel prescription that will actually be deposited: load BK7 / H L 2H L H L H L 2H L H L H L 2H L H L / Air at $\lambda_0=550\,\text{nm}$. Verify yourself that it contains exactly 18 layers, in substrate-to-air order. This architecture is mandatory: it must neither be shortened nor replaced by a symmetric cavity.

Mandatory manual measurement. With a $0.1\,\text{nm}$ spectral step, read from the cursor: (a) the maximum $T_{max}$ and its wavelength, (b) the $T_{max}/2$ level, (c) the two intersections $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, (d) the width $\lambda_2-\lambda_1$, (e) the half-maximum centre $(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)/2$. Use box zoom to refine each intersection, then Reset view to return to the full spectrum.

This prescription contains three $2H$ spacers, hence three coupled cavities: that is what makes it a multicavity filter. Its passband is far wider than the single-cavity filters of Q21 — do not transfer their expected scale — and above all its top is flat and rippled. You will find several near-equal maxima there, and the highest one can jump from one to another after a tiny change in the stack. So it is the half-maximum centre (e), not the position of the maximum, that locates the passband reliably; that is the figure you will reuse in Exercises IX and X.

🔍 Method self-check: measure at half the window height ($T_{max}/2$), never at 50% of the scale. This filter has three cavities: its top is flat and rippled — it is not a peak. Repeat at 0.5 nm and 2 nm steps, compare both intersections, and quantify the reading uncertainty caused by the step.
🎯 Deliverable to keep: export the nominal CSV and the spectrum image. This same 18-layer stack is reused without architectural change in Exercises VIII to X, then manufactured at Fresnel. Without this file, the rest of the project cannot be completed with full traceability.

🎯 Deposited at Fresnel
Direct entry — prerequisites: retain the nominal Q22 file and understand a cavity passband, a merit function and parameter locking. Exercises VI and VII are required; V is useful for Q24.
📋 Component specification
• Measured passband: 535 – 565 nm
• Rejection band 1: 460 – 500 nm
• Rejection band 2: 620 – 680 nm

$Q_r=\dfrac{2\,\overline{T}_{535-565}}{\overline{T}_{460-500}+\overline{T}_{620-680}}$
Q23★★★

Nominal audit and manual $Q_r$ calculation: reopen the Q22 filter, in the same substrate-to-superstrate deposition order.

⚠️ The analyzer does not compute $Q_r$. It only gives you measurements: the average of R, T, or A over a band you define, at a step you choose. It affects neither the optimizer nor Monte-Carlo. The rejection factor is a derived quantity: first build it yourself; the BFGS merit will then use it with fixed bands.
  • In the analyzer, select only T, enter the three specification bands with a 0.5 nm step, and record the three $\overline{T}$ averages. The R and A choices support other measurements; they never assign a role in a merit.
  • Compute $Q_r$ yourself from those three numbers, by hand and then in Excel from the exported CSV. Both routes must agree.
  • Check the effect of the step: repeat the same averages at 0.1 nm and then at 2 nm. Which of the three bands is the most sensitive to the step, and why?
  • Complete the nominal audit — number of layers, number of materials, $d_{min}$, $d_{max}$, total thickness — by computing it from the layer table. The "check my audit" button is only there to verify your result after you have established it.
🧠 The decisive experiment: keep the 535–565 nm passband and 620–680 nm upper rejection band, then recompute $Q_r$ three times, changing only the lower rejection band: 400–500, then 430–500, then the prescribed 460–500. By how much does $Q_r$ vary? Relate this to the actual spectrum. What is a rejection specification worth if it does not state its bands?
Note: the 400–500 nm band extends beyond the computed range (430–700 nm) and the software warns you about it. That warning is part of the answer — say what an average computed over a truncated band is worth.
🔍 Self-check: your manual and Excel calculations must agree within rounding. Check that all three bands are fully covered, that the measured quantity is T, and that the denominator is the mean of both rejection values, not their sum.
Q24★★★

Is there really anything to optimize?

The question is not rhetorical. Start by measuring $Q_r$ and $\overline{T}_{pass}$ before any optimization and compare them with the two requirements of the specification. If the deposited prescription already meets them, and by a wide margin, say so: that is a result, not a failure on your part. You then have to answer a more interesting question: what is the point of optimizing a component that already passes? Look at the margin — by how much does the nominal exceed the threshold, and does that margin survive the deposition errors of Q25? That, rather than the nominal value, is where the three free layers earn their keep.

  • Switch to 🎓 Student mode, exercise VIII: the Maximize Qr merit is selected automatically there. Its formula already uses exactly the 460–500, 535–565, and 620–680 nm bands; the band analyzer remains an independent measurement instrument.
  • Set the cleanup threshold to 2 nm: it is also BFGS's lower bound. Record this constraint in the design dossier; a numerical optimum below it is not admissible.
  • Choose Specific layers, then allow layers 16, 17, and 18 only. Check in the table that layers 1 to 15 are fixed. This constraint belongs to the component that will be deposited and must never be relaxed.
  • Run BFGS and record $Q_r$, $\overline{T}_{pass}$, and the three thicknesses before/after. Verify that the layer count, ordering, and first fifteen thicknesses are strictly unchanged.
  • Test reproducibility by reloading the prescription and rerunning BFGS with identical settings. Explain why identical results are expected from a deterministic algorithm.
  • Reject any nominal improvement that creates a layer below $d_{min}$ or lowers passband transmission below its threshold: an isolated $Q_r$ score is not a manufacturing dossier.
Q25★★★

Pre-manufacturing review — robust qualification without supplied results.

  • (a) Keep the Q24 optimized prescription: 18 layers, the first fifteen unchanged, the final three produced by BFGS. Set $d_{min}=1$ nm and write the acceptance thresholds before any simulation.
    Two different thresholds, not to be confused: the 2 nm of Q24 is a lower bound on what the optimizer may propose; the $d_{min}=1$ nm here is the thinnest layer one accepts to deposit, and it is used to sort the Monte-Carlo draws. The first constrains a calculation, the second judges a fabrication. Justify both values in your dossier.
  • (b) For $\Delta d=1$, 2, and 5 nm, first run $N=100$ to check the protocol, then $N=1000$ for the decision. Keep the seed unchanged.
  • (c) For each severity, report yield, 95% Wilson CI, P05($Q_r$), P05($\overline{T}_{pass}$), manufacturability rate, and number of perturbed layers. Monte-Carlo perturbs the 18 deposited layers: locking the first fifteen applied to BFGS only.
  • (d) Plot yield and both adverse percentiles against $\Delta d$. By cautious interpolation, determine the largest $\Delta d$ compatible with the minimum required yield; if the data do not decide, give an interval and propose the additional run needed.
  • (e) Clearly distinguish nominal performance, statistical robustness, and manufacturability. Base the deposition recommendation on confidence bounds, without treating $\Delta d$ as an absolute bound: here $\sigma=\Delta d/2$.
🔬 Evidence requirement: no yield curve or expected value is supplied. Your plot must show the 1, 2, and 5 nm points, 95% CI bars, seed, $N$, thresholds, and error model. A conclusion without a confidence interval or obtained with mismatched settings is inadmissible.

IX. Cleanroom reverse engineering & defect diagnosis ⏱ 45 min indicative

🎯 After deposition
Direct entry — prerequisites: have the Q22 nominal spectrum and, if possible, the measured filter spectrum; distinguish spectral shift, shape distortion and transmission loss. Exercises VII and VIII are required for full reverse engineering.
Real cleanroom scenario: the Institut Fresnel deposition team has manufactured your bandpass filter. The spectrophotometer readout shows a shift of about $8\,\text{nm}$ towards the red and a loss of peak transmission. Your reference is not the $\lambda_0 = 550\,\text{nm}$ setting, but the half-maximum centre you measured yourself in Q22: that is what the discrepancy is measured against. So start by retrieving that reading.
Q26★★★

Shifted-window diagnosis. Reproduce three defect scenarios in the simulator and compare their spectral signatures:

  • (a) a uniform scaling of every thickness (multiply them all by the same factor);
  • (b) an error on the central $2H$ spacer alone (layer 9);
  • (c) an error on a single mirror layer — use layer 5, so that every pair compares the same thing.

Which one produces a global shift with the least distortion of the window shape? What multiplicative factor is needed to reproduce the observed $8\,\text{nm}$? Convert it into a thickness deviation on the thickest layer, then compare this systematic deviation with the $\Delta d$ severity studied in Q25. Explain why the independent Gaussian model of Q25 cannot, by itself, declare this common bias covered.

Finally, explain why transmission loss alone does not identify a unique layer.

Q27★★★
Metrology and traceability: relate the layer-to-layer random error, set by $\Delta d$, to the spread seen across the plotted draws. Derive an optical-thickness tolerance and a resolution requirement from adverse percentiles, not from the nominal result alone. Then translate them into a quartz-monitor recommendation while explicitly listing the missing process data: tooling factor, density, Z-ratio, rate stability, calibration history, and material-specific validation. No quantified instrument setting may be issued without these data — state this explicitly in your conclusion.

🎨 Open workshop — design your own component Not graded · self-service

The ten main exercises imposed specifications on you. Here, you write them. The simulator accepts any spectral target: it becomes a reusable design tool well beyond this lab — for a project, an internship, or simply to test an idea.

🚀 Declare the target — 30 seconds

  1. Press D — or click 🎯 Free design in the header.
  2. Click + Segment, then translate each requirement into one row of the table.
  3. Enter λ min, λ max, T/R/A quantity, angle, polarization, target and weight.
  4. Save it (↓): this is your specification, and it must not move during design.

⚙️ What the merit computes

The score is a weighted RMSE between your computed response and your target, over the declared segments only:

$$\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_i w_i\left(X_i-C_i\right)^2}{\sum_i w_i}}$$

Anything you do not cover is "don't care" and does not enter the score. The weight $w_i$ lets you state that one band matters more than another. This is exactly the structure of an industrial specification.

🛠️ The designer's method — this is a craft, not a click

Declaring a target and pressing "Optimize" almost never produces a good component. Real design is an iterative cycle that you must run yourself, and it is not finished until the feasibility study.

1
Choose a starting solution — never at random.

The optimizer is local: it descends into whichever valley you drop it in. Start from a structure that physics justifies — a quarter-wave for antireflection, (HL)^n for a mirror, (HL)^n 2H (LH)^n for a bandpass — centred on the right wavelength. A good initialization beats any algorithm.

2
Refine the thicknesses with Quasi-Newton (BFGS).

Run the Quasi-Newton (BFGS) optimizer with key O. It adjusts thicknesses at constant structure: it will never create a layer, it only exploits the ones you give it. Record the merit before and after.

3
Enrich the structure: needle → optimize cycles.

When BFGS stops progressing, the structure is the limit, not the thicknesses. Run 📍 Analyze Needle (key N): the $\pi(z)$ diagnostic tells you where to insert an ultra-thin layer of the other material. Insert, then immediately re-optimize with BFGS. Repeat.

When to stop? When a full cycle no longer lowers the merit significantly, or when the needle finds no positive maximum. Keep a log: layer count and merit at every cycle. The curve "merit versus number of layers" is a design result.

4
Clean the stack — the step everyone forgets.

After a few cycles your stack contains 0.1 nm slices and adjacent layers of the same material. None of them is depositable. Two tools:

  • 🧹 Clean up button (top of panel 2. Layer stack): set a threshold; it removes every thinner layer and merges adjacent layers of the same material. It then reports how many layers you saved and how much merit you lost.
  • Row-by-row deletion: the Action column of the layer table, to remove one specific layer you judge useless.

Then re-optimize: the remaining thicknesses must absorb what was removed. You will often be surprised — a 7-layer stack can shrink to 2 with no measurable loss. A layer that costs nothing to delete was doing nothing.

5
Arbitrate: performance versus complexity.

Every added layer costs machine time, one more error source and a scrap risk. Compare your successive designs: how many merit points does the 8th layer buy? If the answer is "almost none", the engineering decision is to drop it. The best design is not the highest performing one: it is the simplest one that meets the specification.

6
Close with the feasibility study — otherwise the work is not done.

A design exists only if it can be deposited. Apply the lab protocol to your component:

  • Nominal audit: number of layers and materials, $d_{min}$, $d_{max}$, total thickness. A layer below the deposition threshold disqualifies the design.
  • Statistical qualification: Assess robustness, $N=1000$. Report yield, 95% CI, adverse percentile and manufacturability rate.
  • Common-bias sensitivity: it is almost always the dominant factor. Determine the calibration tolerance to require from the process.
  • Decision: qualified, conditionally qualified or not qualified, with your assumptions and at least two model limitations.

An "optimized" design without a feasibility study is not a deliverable: it is a draft.

The cycle in one line: physical start → BFGS → (needle → BFGS)ⁿ → cleanup → BFGS → arbitration → feasibility. Record the merit at every step: that is your design trace, and it is what gets assessed.
⚠️ Three traps this lab taught you to avoid — they apply here too:
  • Do not specify what physics forbids. A T = 100% target over 400–1000 nm on an absorbing substrate is unreachable: the optimizer will converge to a poor compromise without warning you.
  • Leave don't-care zones. A sharp transition needs room: if you specify 100% at 550 nm and 0% at 555 nm, no finite stack will achieve it.
  • A good nominal score alone is worth nothing. Always run the statistical qualification before declaring a design good.

🎯 Six challenges, from simplest to most demanding

★☆☆ Cold mirror. $R \ge 95\%$ at every point of $700-850$ nm, and $\overline{T} \ge 85\%$ over $420-650$ nm. How many layers do you need? Hint: a quarter-wave stack centred at 775 nm gets there; find the minimum count.
★☆☆ Single-colour antireflection. Aim for $\overline{R} \le 1\%$ over $530-570$ nm only, with ZnS/YF₃ on BK7. Compare with the broadband design of Q16: the price of bandwidth. You will not reach 0.2%: compare $n_L=1.469$ with the ideal index $\sqrt{n_{BK7}}=1.232$ from Q10, and conclude on what limits you — the algorithm or the materials?
★★☆ Notch filter. Try to block $T \le 1\%$ over $525-545$ nm while keeping $\overline{T} \ge 80\%$ everywhere else in the visible. Measure the width you actually obtain below 10% transmission: it will not drop below ≈ 150 nm, however many layers you add. Relate that limit to the index contrast of Q4, and say which material pair would be needed for a 20 nm notch.
★★☆ 50/50 beamsplitter. $R=T=50\%$ over $450-650$ nm, target RMSE $\le 18$ points. A non-extreme target is harder than an extreme one: $R=0$ and $R=1$ are natural points of interference, $R=50\%$ is not. Check that adding layers beyond 3 barely helps, and explain why.
★★★ Your own, qualified. Write a full specification, meet it, then qualify it ($N=1000$). Submit the Q28–Q32 decision sheet on your component.
★★★ The duel. Have another team design a target, swap JSON files, and qualify each other's design. Defend or refute their conclusion.

Keep your work: the ↓ and ↑ buttons in the editor export and re-import your target as JSON. A saved specification can be reused, passed to a classmate, or attached to a report as supporting evidence.
